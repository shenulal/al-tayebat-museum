مدينة الطيبات العالمية للعلوم والمعرفة

Where Jeddah keeps its memory, room by room.

Twelve houses built the way old Jeddah built them: coral stone, lime, and teak balconies carved to catch the sea wind. Inside, three hundred rooms hold the manuscripts, coins, dress and daily life of the Kingdom.

حكاية البيت

A private collection that grew into a city.

For most of the twentieth century, Sheikh Abdul Raouf Hasan Khalil collected what Jeddah was throwing away. Carved doors from houses coming down in Al Balad. Ottoman coins. Qur'an leaves. The wedding dress of a Hijazi bride, the bisht of an Al Ahsa merchant, the silver of the south. When the collection outgrew every room he owned, he did what a Jeddawi would do: he built a neighbourhood for it.

The city stands in Al Faisaliyah, a few kilometres from the old port, and borrows its whole vocabulary from there. Walls of coral stone from the Red Sea reef, finished with النورة lime so they stay cool and white. Teak rawasheen stacked up the facades, their lattices turned so a woman could watch the street without being seen. A green-domed mosque at the centre, because every Hijazi quarter has one.

Walk it like a quarter, not a museum. Houses open onto houses. One room holds Qur'ans and a 1495 copy of Nezami's Khamsa; the next, the first Saudi riyal; the next, a scale model of the Holy Mosque at Makkah with the pilgrims' route drawn around it. Most people plan for two hours and stay for four.

The Kingdom's regions each have a house here: Jeddah, Makkah, Madinah, Riyadh, Al Ahsa, Asir and the north, furnished the way those cities lived.

الشعار

The mark

The emblem is the city as you meet it from the street, cut down to what nobody else has: a Hijazi house with its rawasheen stacked up the wall, the pointed door, the crenellated roof, the minaret with its green cap and the green dome of the mosque beside it. The dashed line beneath is the route through the houses. It is drawn in teak and dome green, the two colours of the place, and it stays legible at the size of a ticket stamp.

Files: horizontal lockup, stacked, compact, mark, on teak, favicon.

بيت التراث الإسلامي

The House of Islamic Heritage

Qur'an manuscripts from the early centuries, astrolabes and brass instruments, and the Khamsa of Nezami copied in 1495. At the centre, a model of the Holy Mosque with the Hajj route around it, built so that pilgrims could show their families where they had walked.

Manuscripts · Calligraphy · Instruments of the sky · The Makkah model

بيت التراث السعودي

The Houses of the Kingdom

Each region keeps its own house. The Jeddah rooms are a merchant's home with its majlis and kitchen; the Najd rooms are mud and tamarisk; Asir arrives in painted colour. The costume pavilion runs north to south: the zaboun and mahram of the Hijaz, the gilded bisht of Al Ahsa, the silver and garlands of the south.

Jeddah · Makkah · Madinah · Riyadh · Al Ahsa · Asir · The North

جناح العملات والتراث

Coins, paper and the founding of the state

Hashemite and Ottoman coinage, the first Saudi riyal, early banknotes, and the rooms given to King Abdulaziz: weapons, letters, and the objects of a court that was still a camp.

Currency · Weapons · The King Abdulaziz rooms

بيت التراث العالمي

The House of International Heritage

Jeddah was a port before it was anything else. These rooms hold what came through it: Chinese porcelain, Indian textiles, African carving, European clocks, the trade of a Red Sea city that fed Makkah.

Porcelain · Textiles · Clocks · The trade of the Red Sea

قاعات العلوم والمعرفة

Science and knowledge

The halls that give the city its name: the sciences of the Islamic world set beside the technology that changed the Kingdom in a single lifetime, from the first radio to the first oil well.

Astronomy · Medicine · The Kingdom's first century

مفردات البيت الحجازي

Words you will need inside.

Rawasheen رواشين
The projecting wooden balconies, carved and latticed, that cover a Hijazi facade. They shade the wall, catch the breeze and let the house look out without being looked into. The museum's are carved in teak, like the old ones in Al Balad.
Manjur منجور
The turned-wood lattice itself, the pattern of small spindles inside a roshan. Count the spindles; the finer they are, the richer the house.
Hajar manqabi حجر منقبي
Coral limestone cut from the reef at Manqabah, the stone of every old house in Jeddah. Soft when quarried, it hardens in air and keeps rooms cool.
Nura نورة
Lime plaster, burnt and slaked, then washed white over the coral. It is why the houses glow at dusk and why the city here is white.
Majlis مجلس
The sitting room where guests are received: cushions along the walls, coffee in the corner. Several of the houses keep theirs furnished, and the courtyard majlis still serves tea.
Mangad منقد
The raised seat under the roshan where the owner sat to catch the wind and watch the lane. The best seat in a Hijazi house.

Good to know

Fifteen minutes from the Corniche, twenty from King Abdulaziz airport, with free parking at the main gate. Guided tours run in Arabic and English and come with the Guided Heritage ticket.

The Makkah model and the manuscript rooms fill up on busy afternoons; go there first. Photography without flash is welcome in most rooms.

The houses are on several levels. Ask at the gate for the step-free route. The mosque under the green dome is open for prayer, and the courtyard majlis serves tea through the day.

أسئلة الزوار

Questions visitors ask.

How long does a visit take?

Two to three hours for the main houses. People who want all three hundred rooms often come back; your booking reference lets you in a second time within seven days.

Is it a place for children?

Very much. Under-sixes enter free, and the Makkah model, the coin rooms and the costume pavilion are the rooms children remember. Pushchairs can follow the ground-floor route.

Can I change my date?

Yes, up to 24 hours before your hour. Open your confirmation email and choose a new date, or call the museum with your booking reference.

What should I wear?

Modest dress is appreciated in the mosque and prayer rooms. Elsewhere, dress for Jeddah. The lanes are shaded but warm, and there is a lot of walking.

Are the tours in English?

Yes. Guided Heritage tours run daily in Arabic and English, and the audio guide also speaks Urdu and French.
